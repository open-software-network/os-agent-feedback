PetSmart × EpodeAI Commerce Optimization Report · 2026-08-06

AI agents are already shopping PetSmart. Right now, they leave nothing behind.

1 · The narrative

A growing share of PetSmart traffic isn't people — it's ChatGPT and Claude, shopping on a customer's behalf. Today that visit looks like anonymous scraping: the agent reads marketing copy, makes its best guess, answers the customer somewhere you can't see, and disappears. You don't learn what the household needed, you can't tell whether you won or lost the recommendation, and if the shopper ever clicks through, they arrive as a stranger.

Epode gives the agent a machine-readable version of the storefront that negotiates like your best associate would — asking what the household actually needs and answering from catalog truth. In exchange, PetSmart gets the whole story: the needs, the journey, a first-party customer, and the sale — measured.

2 · Before and after

Without Epode

  • Agent visits look like scraping — no journey, no intent, no session
  • The agent guesses fit from marketing copy; the wrong product gets recommended, and the sale (or the return) is yours either way
  • A click-through lands an anonymous visitor; nothing the agent learned survives the handoff
  • Next visit, the shopper is a stranger again
  • Agent-assisted revenue is invisible in attribution

With Epode

  • Every agent visit is a structured journey — the expressed needs (pet mix, food motivation, budget) on a step-by-step timeline
  • Ranking runs on your catalog evidence; a wrong-product entry gets redirected to the right product instead of a lost sale
  • One click mints a first-party identity, and the household's traits arrive as provenance-labeled signals on a customer record
  • Return visits personalize from stored context — decisions recorded against the exact signals used
  • Conversions attribute back to the agent journey; everything exports to your warehouse

Both runs below ended the same way: a customer record with remembered traits, a personalized return visit, and an attributed conversion — reached through two different doors.

3 · The how — two real journeys

Each journey started as a customer message handed to a real Claude agent — nothing else. One asks for PetSmart by name; the other found a PetSmart listing on their own and asks whether it would work. Everything shown is what actually happened; the customer's browser is driven by a script, every decision is the agent's own.

The customer asks their agent to shop PetSmart by name.

Customer → agentHey, we need a new feeder for our pets. We have two 3-year-old cats and a dog, and one of the cats is SUPER food-motivated — she finishes her bowl in seconds and then pushes the others out of the way and eats their food too. It's becoming a real problem, the other two are losing weight. We'd like to stay around $200. Can you look at PetSmart and find something that would actually work for us?

The crawl

The agent fetches the storefront and gets a machine-readable graph

Given only the prompt and the store URL, the agent fetched the homepage with an honest agent User-Agent and received the experience guide instead of HTML — an entry into the need-negotiation graph, with the traversal rules spelled out. It followed them exactly: nine URLs opened, every one supplied by the merchant, none constructed.

2

Disclosure and ranking

It disclosed four needs — and chose their strengths itself

The agent expressed pets-cats-and-dog and motivation-one-food-motivated as hard constraints (the customer's own words), encoded the budget as a $200 target rather than a ceiling because the customer said “around $200”, and inferred priority-functional-fit from “something that would actually work”. The merchant ranked one exact match — the SmartTag RFID Multi-Pet Feeder, every dimension backed by catalog evidence — and five near misses, each with the constraint it violates.

The recommendation

The customer gets their answer — with the product link inside

The agent's reply is grounded entirely in the merchant's evidence: the one exact match, why it solves the food-stealing problem, and why the runner-up fails. The link it hands over is an ordinary product page URL, carrying the journey reference.

Now you know them

PetSmart now has that information — as a customer record

The household's traits arrive as structured signals — no permission round-trip, each value labeled with where it came from and how sure the agent is. In the dashboard they live on a customer record: “Bubbly Raven” is an auto-generated label for the shopper's anonymous first-party browser ID (nobody knows their name), with the agent journey and the browser visit linked to it. Just as telling is what the agent chose not to send: it could have inferred pet.life_stage from “3-year-old cats”, and withheld it on its own judgment.

pet.household_mix cats_and_dog agent_reports_user_statement · confidence 0.98 the customer said it outright — marked remembered: a durable household fact
pet.food_motivation one_food_motivated agent_reports_user_statement · confidence 0.98 the core problem, in the customer's words — marked remembered

Values are constrained to the merchant-defined catalog, so prompts, transcripts, and PII are impossible by schema. Queryable in the API and ready for warehouse export.

5

The click

The customer opens the link — PetSmart drops its cookie and session

Opening the product page mints the signed first-party ps_visitor and ps_session cookies and ties them to the agent journey — the moment anonymous AI traffic becomes the first-party identity behind that customer record. The page carries Epode's customer-context contract, already submit-ready with the field catalog inline; the agent answers through PetSmart's own origin in the same breath.

6

The payoff

Next visit, the homepage knows the household

Same URL that served an anonymous grid an hour earlier — but this visitor's cookie now resolves to stored context. The hero greets the two-cats-and-a-dog household and features the feeder; a personalization decision is recorded against the retrieved signals. The customer clicked through and hit Add to cart — a conversion outcome recorded against that decision.

The listing entry

The agent lands on the product page — and gets the graph, not HTML

Same product URL a human would open, but the agent's honest User-Agent gets the machine-readable item node: catalog facts, the seller's marketing claim kept attributed and unverified, and an evaluation graph starting right here. No cookies are minted for agents — identities belong to human browsers. This is reverse traversal: the storefront home was never visited.

2

Reverse traversal

The graph answers the customer's actual question: unsuitable

The agent expressed the household from the item — cats and a dog, one food-motivated eater, $200 target — and asked for the fit verdict. The merchant's own catalog answers honestly: hard conflict on pets (this feeder is listed for one-cat or one-dog homes), food-stealing prevention not specified, and the only match is price. The verdict is “unsuitable”, with every line backed by catalog evidence — the merchant tells the truth about its own product.

3

The redirect

Alternatives rank the right product for the same needs

A non-suitable verdict unlocks alternatives, ranked against the same expressed needs. The SmartTag RFID Multi-Pet Feeder is the only zero-conflict option (utilityScore 12); every other feeder is excluded with the exact household or motivation conflict it fails on. The wrong-product entry didn't lose the sale — the graph redirected it.
